Two more worked examples

1. A pH that is not a whole number. 0.020 M HCl: [H+] = 0.020 mol/L, pH = −log10(0.020) = 1.70. Going back, pH 3.40 means [H+] = 10−3.40 = 4.0 × 10−4 mol/L.

2. A base. 0.0010 M NaOH gives [OH−] = 1.0 × 10−3 mol/L. Then [H+] = 10−14 / 10−3 = 10−11 mol/L, and pH = 11. The basic SC-1 wafer clean works in this range.

Three mistakes to avoid

  1. "pH 2 has twice the H+ of pH 4." Each pH unit is a factor of 10: it is 100 times.
  2. "Dilute an acid enough and it becomes basic." Water's own H+ (10−7 mol/L) takes over; the pH approaches 7 and never passes it.
  3. "At equilibrium the reaction stops, and the amounts are equal." Both directions continue at equal rates; the amounts are constant, not equal.

Practice

Work on paper. The first four repeat the class; the rest go one step further.

1. Find the pH of 0.0010 M HCl.

Answer3.00.

2. 0.010 M HCl is diluted 10 times. What is the new pH?

Answer[H+] = 0.0010 M, pH 3.00: up by 1.

3. A sample has pH 2. Find [OH−] at 25 °C.

Answer10−12 mol/L.

4. In the model A ⇌ B with K = 0.5, the total is 12. What are A and B at equilibrium?

AnswerB = 0.5 A and A + B = 12, so A = 8, B = 4.

5. Find the pH of 0.020 M HCl.

Answer1.70.

6. A rinse bath reads pH 3.40. What is [H+]?

Answer10−3.40 = 4.0 × 10−4 mol/L.

7. Find the pH of 0.0010 M NaOH at 25 °C.

Answer[OH−] = 10−3, so [H+] = 10−11: pH 11.

8. In the model, start from A = 8, B = 4 and add 8 units of B instead of A. Where does the new equilibrium lie?

AnswerTotal 20 and B/A = 0.5 again: A = 13.33, B = 6.67. The ratio returns to K whatever you add.
