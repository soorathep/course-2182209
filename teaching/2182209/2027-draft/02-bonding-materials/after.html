Two more worked examples

1. Si atoms in 1 cm³ of a wafer. The Si unit cell is a cube with edge a = 0.5431 nm = 0.5431 × 10−7 cm, and it holds 8 atoms (8 corners × 1/8 + 6 faces × 1/2 + 4 inside).

Cell volume = (0.5431 × 10−7 cm)3 = 1.602 × 10−22 cm³

Atoms per cm³ = 8 / (1.602 × 10−22) = 4.99 × 1022

Classes 3 and 6 use this number.

2. A narrower wire. A Cu wire is 1 mm long, 50 nm wide and 100 nm thick. Convert to meters first:

A = (50 × 10−9 m)(100 × 10−9 m) = 5.0 × 10−15 m²

R = ρL/A = (1.68 × 10−8 Ω·m)(1.0 × 10−3 m) / (5.0 × 10−15 m²) = 3360 Ω

The area is a quarter of the class example (100 nm × 200 nm), so R is four times 840 Ω. This is why shrinking wires is a real problem for chip makers.

Three mistakes to avoid

  1. "It contains ions, so it conducts." Solid NaCl has ions but does not conduct: they are fixed in place.
  2. "Covalent means insulator." Graphite is covalent and conducts along its layers; Si is covalent and is a semiconductor.
  3. "A thinner wire has a higher resistivity." ρ belongs to the material. A thinner wire has a higher resistance, because A is smaller. (In real wires only a few tens of nanometers wide, ρ itself also rises, a topic for later courses.)

Practice

Work on paper. The first four repeat the class; the rest go one step further. χ: H 2.1, Al 1.5, Si 1.8, Cu 1.9, Cl 3.0, O 3.5. ρ: Cu 1.68 × 10−8, Al 2.65 × 10−8 Ω·m; SiO2 about 1016 Ω·m.

1. Classify the bonds Al–O, H–Cl and Cu–Cu.

AnswerAl–O: Δχ = 2.0, ionic by the guide (real Al2O3 has some covalent character). H–Cl: 0.9, polar covalent. Cu–Cu: two metal atoms, metallic.

2. Why does molten NaCl conduct when solid NaCl does not?

AnswerIn the melt the Na+ and Cl− ions can move, so they carry the current. In the solid they are held in the crystal.

3. W has a body-centered cubic cell: one atom at each corner and one in the center. How many atoms belong to one cell?

Answer8 × 1/8 + 1 = 2.

4. Fused silica has a density of 2.20 g/cm³ and quartz 2.65 g/cm³. By what percentage is fused silica less dense?

Answer(2.65 − 2.20) / 2.65 = 0.17, about 17%.

5. A Cu wire is 1 mm long, 50 nm wide and 100 nm thick. Find R.

AnswerA = 5.0 × 10−15 m²; R = 1.68 × 10−8 × 1.0 × 10−3 / 5.0 × 10−15 = 3360 Ω.

6. An Al wire is 200 nm thick, like the class Cu wire. How wide must it be to have the same R as the 100 nm wide Cu wire?

AnswerR is the same when ρ/A is the same, so w(Al) = 100 nm × 2.65/1.68 = 158 nm.

7. A wire is made twice as long and twice as wide (same thickness). What happens to R?

AnswerNothing: L doubles and A doubles, so ρL/A is unchanged.

8. A SiO2 layer 10 nm thick separates two 1 µm × 1 µm pads at 1 V. Estimate the current. About how long is it between electrons? (e = 1.602 × 10−19 C)

AnswerR = 1016 × 10 × 10−9 / 10−12 = 1020 Ω, so I = 1 V / 1020 Ω = 10−20 A. One electron every 1.602 × 10−19 / 10−20 = 16 s. A real 10 nm layer leaks more than this, because electrons can tunnel through very thin insulators.
