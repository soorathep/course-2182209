The question for Class 6 3 min

Class 2 placed Si between Cu and SiO2 in resistivity and said doping can move it. Class 6 explains why Si sits there, and calculates how much a tiny amount of P or B changes it: one P atom per five million Si atoms lowers the resistivity about half a million times. This is the chemistry behind every transistor.

Energy levels become bands 6 min

An electron in an atom can have only certain energies. When very many atoms join into a solid, these levels crowd together into ranges of allowed energy called bands. A band is a range of energies, not a layer in space.

  • The valence band holds the bonding electrons.
  • The conduction band is the next band up, normally empty.
  • The band gap Eg is the energy range between them where no electron can be. It is measured in electronvolts: 1 eV = 1.602 × 10−19 J.

To carry current, an electron must move into an empty state close in energy. In a metal such as Cu, a band is only partly full, so empty states sit right next to filled ones. In Si (Eg = 1.12 eV), heat lifts a few electrons across the gap. In SiO2 (about 9 eV), almost none cross.

Electrons and holes 6 min

When an electron leaves a Si–Si bond and moves into the conduction band, it leaves behind a bond short of one electron: a hole. A neighboring bond electron can jump into the hole, so the hole moves the other way. A hole behaves like a mobile positive charge.

So Si has two kinds of carrier: electrons, with concentration n, and holes, with concentration p, both in cm−3 (per cubic centimeter). In pure Si at room temperature n = p = ni = 1.45 × 1010 cm−3. Compare this with 5 × 1022 Si atoms per cm³ (Class 2): only about 3 atoms in 1013 release an electron.

Doping, in one paragraph 5 min

Si has 4 valence electrons and makes 4 bonds. Replace one Si atom with P (5 valence electrons): 4 make bonds and the fifth is easily freed. P is a donor, and the Si becomes n-type, with electrons as the majority carrier. Replace Si with B (3 valence electrons): one bond is short, which makes a hole. B is an acceptor, and the Si becomes p-type, with holes as the majority carrier.

The product rule you will use: n × p = ni2, the same kind of rule as [H+][OH−] = Kw in Class 5

Check yourself 5 min

Write your answers first, then open each one.

1. How many valence electrons do B, Si and P have?

Answer3, 4, 5.

2. Is a band a layer inside the material?

AnswerNo. It is a range of energies that electrons may have.

3. Which has the larger band gap, Si or SiO2?

AnswerSiO2, about 9 eV against 1.12 eV.

4. What is a hole, and what charge does it behave as?

AnswerA bond missing an electron; it moves like a +e charge.

5. Does P make Si n-type or p-type?

Answern-type: P is a donor and gives electrons.

6. [H+] = 10−3 M. What is [OH−]? (Class 5)

Answer10−11 M. Today the same product rule links electrons and holes.
