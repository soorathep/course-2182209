Two more worked examples

1. p-type Si. Si doped with NA = 1016 cm−3 of B. Each B gives one hole, so p = 1016 cm−3, and n = ni2/p = 2.1 × 1020 / 1016 = 2.1 × 104 cm−3.

ρ = 1 / [e(nμn + pμp)] ≈ 1 / (1.602 × 10−19 × 1016 × 480) = 1.30 Ω·cm

That is about 2.8 times the 0.46 Ω·cm of n-type Si at the same doping, because holes move more slowly (μp = 480 against μn = 1350 cm²/(V·s)).

2. More doping. 1017 cm−3 of P: ρ ≈ 1 / (1.602 × 10−19 × 1017 × 1350) = 0.046 Ω·cm in the class model. Ten times more dopant, ten times lower ρ. Real Si at this doping is somewhat higher, because mobility falls when there are many dopant ions.

Three mistakes to avoid

  1. "n-type Si is negatively charged." Each free electron is balanced by a fixed P+ ion. The wafer is neutral.
  2. "The band gap alone gives the conductivity." Conductivity also needs the number of carriers and their mobility.
  3. "Doping adds both carriers." Raising the majority lowers the minority: n p stays ni2.

Practice

Work on paper. The first four repeat the class; the rest go one step further. ni = 1.45 × 1010 cm−3 (ni2 = 2.1 × 1020 cm−6); μn = 1350, μp = 480 cm²/(V·s); e = 1.602 × 10−19 C; 4.99 × 1022 Si atoms per cm³.

1. Classify P and B in Si: donor or acceptor, n- or p-type, majority carrier.

AnswerP: donor, n-type, electrons. B: acceptor, p-type, holes.

2. ND = 1016 cm−3. Find n and p.

Answern = 1016; p = 2.1 × 1020 / 1016 = 2.1 × 104 cm−3.

3. A model has donor ions +5e and free electrons −5e. Total charge?

AnswerZero.

4. Why must Cu be kept away from the Si where transistors sit?

AnswerTiny amounts of unwanted atoms change Si's carriers, as dopants do, but out of control.

5. Find p, n and ρ for Si with 1016 cm−3 of B.

Answerp = 1016, n = 2.1 × 104 cm−3, ρ ≈ 1.30 Ω·cm.

6. What B concentration gives the same ρ as 1016 cm−3 of P?

AnswerNeed p μp = 1016 × 1350, so p = 1016 × 1350/480 = 2.8 × 1016 cm−3.

7. 1015 cm−3 of P. Find the P atoms per Si atom and ρ.

Answer1015 / 4.99 × 1022 = 2.0 × 10−8, one per 50 million. ρ ≈ 1/(1.602 × 10−19 × 1015 × 1350) = 4.6 Ω·cm.

8. In pure Si, ni rises steeply with temperature. Why is doped Si better for a device that must work from 0 °C to 80 °C?

AnswerIn doped Si the majority carrier count is set by the dopant (n ≈ ND), which does not change with temperature, so the current stays steady. In pure Si the carrier count, and so the current, would change many times over.
